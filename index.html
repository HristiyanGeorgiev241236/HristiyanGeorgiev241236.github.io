Y2·B · Untangling plant roots, part 1 of 3

Engineering robust plant phenotyping

NPEC photographs thousands of Arabidopsis seedlings on agar plates. Scientists need each plant's main-root length and root-tip position so a robot can inoculate it. On real plates roots cross, seeds fail to germinate and condensation blurs the view. The brief asked for one segmentation model; I ended up with five working together.

WhenNov 2025 – Jan 2026
TeamIndividual
ClientNetherlands Plant Eco-phenotyping Centre
The challenge

Measure the main root of every seedling on NPEC's plates, where roots cross, seeds float and condensation blurs the image, and guide a robot to each root tip.

My approach

Five models working together: SegFormers for shoots, roots and seeds, a second seed model combined by agreement, and an RT-DETR detector trained on labels my own models produced. Then graph-based root measurement and a PID-controlled robot.

Achievement

Detection precision 0.998 and recall 0.973 on 111 plants, about 11% error on root length, and a robot that reaches each root tip within 1 mm.

The pipeline, step by step

NPEC images are confidential, so the pipeline is described rather than shown.

0.998detection precision (111 plants)
0.973detection recall
0.984mAP@50
≈11%sMAPE on root length, course Kaggle

Three failures that shaped it

  • Labelling by hand was too slow. Painting every root pixel was out of the question. I wrote a tool that marks the highest and lowest root points of each plant and joins them with a shortest-path search through the root mask, then checked and repaired more than 100 images instead of drawing them.
  • Seed predictions were noisy. Extra training data made the seed model worse. Going through it, I found it was really two different datasets merged into one. I trained one model on stand-alone seeds and one on the combined data, then kept only the pixels both agreed on. That gave the cleanest seed detection of anything I tried.
  • Segmentation can't separate crossing plants. A mask says "root", not "whose root". I used my own segmentation models to auto-label a detection dataset, then trained an RT-DETR detector to draw one box per plant.

Then the robot

The second half moved the result onto a simulated Opentrons OT-2 liquid handler. I mapped its work envelope, tuned a PID controller that reaches targets within 1 mm, and trained a PPO reinforcement-learning controller with a curriculum: the goal radius starts at 100 mm and shrinks by 10% each time the agent succeeds 80% of the time. After low-learning-rate fine-tuning it landed within 1 mm in 48 of 100 test episodes, with a best case of 0.21 mm. The PID controller was more reliable, so it drove the final integration, where the vision pipeline finds each root tip and the robot inoculates it.

What I'd do differently. My rule for where the main root starts fits the competition images but not every plant. I proposed adding time: roots grow and scratches don't, so tracking tips across frames would separate signal from noise, with the current pipeline producing the pseudo-labels.

Y2·C · Untangling plant roots, part 2 of 3

Pixel ownership: can colour cues untangle crossing roots?

The weak point of my pipeline was the overlap between plants. About 32% of root biomass falls inside overlapping bounding boxes, where a box can't tell you which plant a pixel belongs to. If I paint what is already known in distinct colours, can a segmentation model carry that ownership into the ambiguous zone?

WhenFeb – Apr 2026
TeamIndividual study
FocusResearch design & statistics
The challenge

Bounding boxes can't tell which plant owns a root pixel where plants overlap, about 32% of root biomass, and no ground-truth labels existed for those crossings.

My approach

Built a synthetic generator with known pixel owners, gave a SegFormer colour-coded context, and tested it against a box-only baseline with paired, non-parametric and bootstrap statistics.

Achievement

IoU in the overlap rose from 0.671 to 0.776 (p ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻⁶, n = 60), with the limits of a synthetic-only test stated clearly.

  • No ground truth existed for who owns which pixel in a real crossing. I built a synthetic generator that takes clean single plants, overlaps them so their roots genuinely cross, and records the true owner of every pixel.
  • Baseline: the best a detector can do on its own, splitting the conflict zone down its middle.
  • Model: a SegFormer that sees each plant's shoot and its roots outside the overlap painted in distinct colours, and must label the uncoloured conflict zone.
  • Analysis: paired comparison on 60 evaluation images, with a paired t-test, a Wilcoxon signed-rank test and a 10,000-sample bootstrap so the conclusion doesn't rest on one assumption.

n = 60 paired images · paired t = 5.22, p ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻⁶ (one-sided) · Wilcoxon p ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁶ · Cohen's dz = 0.67 (medium)

What the result does not show. All evaluation images come from the same synthetic generator as the training data, so the test proves an improvement on that distribution, not on real plates. Real crossings have no per-pixel ownership labels yet, and a 2D image can't resolve roots stacked in depth. The next step I proposed is to hand-label a small set of real conflict zones and measure the gap.

Alongside the study I wrote the data management plan and ethics submission (the NPEC images are proprietary and never left local storage), presented an A0 poster, chaired our journal club and ran the final sprint as Scrum master.

Y2·D · Untangling plant roots, part 3 of 3

Root disentangler in production

Our team turned the detector and the ownership model into a service a lab could run and keep improving. I was the main engineer: 129 of the repository's 222 commits are mine, including the API, inference pipeline, model registry, retraining logic, database layer and most of the tests.

WhenApr – Jun 2026
Team4 people
FocusMLOps
The challenge

Turn research models into a service a lab could run, correct and improve without the developers in the room.

My approach

A tested Python package behind FastAPI, a feedback-and-approval loop, Airflow retraining with a promotion gate, an MLflow model registry and automatic deploys to the campus server.

Achievement

Main engineer in a team of four (129 of 222 commits), with an 85% test-coverage gate and about seven minutes from merge to running server.

The continuous-learning loop
  1. Predictoperator uploads a plate
  2. Feedbackmarks a result right or wrong
  3. Approvesupervisor accepts the sample
  4. Airflowenough samples, or Friday 02:00
  5. Retrain + evaluatelogged to MLflow
  6. Promotion gatebeats the current model, or discard
  7. Hot-swapnew weights live, no restart

The new model serves the next prediction. Every run stores the dataset hash and the IDs of the samples it was trained on.

  • Python package with a FastAPI service and a command-line tool sharing the same code; Streamlit front end with separate pages for operators, scientists and engineers.
  • Backend, inference, front end, MLflow, Airflow, Postgres, MinIO and nginx, each in its own container.
  • The campus server sits behind a VPN, so GitHub couldn't reach it to trigger deploys. Instead, a Watchtower container inside the network polls the image registry and replaces any service whose image changed, with no manual step.

Cancer and gene mutations

The block asked for a Power BI dashboard on a topic of our choice. I chose cancer genetics, with no biology background, and learned the domain while building it: from global incidence and mortality down to brain-tumour patients and their mutations.

WhenSep – Nov 2024
TeamIndividual
DataIARC · TCGA via GDC and cBioPortal
The challenge

Tell a clear story about cancer and gene mutations, a field I had no background in, using a tool that wasn't built for survival analysis.

My approach

Learned the domain while building, combined global IARC statistics with TCGA patient data, and wrote the Kaplan–Meier analysis in R when Power BI couldn't.

Achievement

A dashboard showing that IDH1-mutant patients had a median survival of 8.0 years against 2.2 without the mutation, and how IDH1 and TP53 interact.

  • IDH1 is mutated in about three in four of these tumours, and those patients live far longer: median survival of 8.0 years against 2.2 years without the mutation.
  • TP53 alone tells a less clear story. Its survival curves cross after about eight years, so the dashboard goes on to compare cases with IDH1 only, TP53 only and both, and the copy-number changes (gene amplifications and deletions) that travel with each group.
  • Power BI can't draw Kaplan–Meier curves, which were the core of the story, so I wrote them in R and embedded the scripts in the report.
  • Documented with CRISP-DM, including the limits: restricted access to patient-level data, and survival differences that show association, not cause.

Y1·B

Hospital readmission and length of stay

101,765 diabetic patient encounters from 130 US hospitals. The task was to predict readmission within 30 days and length of stay. The first models looked excellent, which is what made me suspicious.

WhenNov 2024 – Jan 2025
TeamIndividual
DomainHealthcare
The challenge

Predict 30-day readmission and length of stay for 101,765 diabetic patient encounters.

My approach

Questioned results that looked too good, rebalanced the classes, removed features that leak the outcome, and used the dataset's original research paper to decode diagnosis codes.

Achievement

Exposed a 91%-accuracy model that caught only 0.4% of readmissions, built realistic models instead, and gave hospitals a concrete recommendation: test HbA1c consistently.

91%accuracy of the first readmission models
0.4%of actual readmissions the logistic regression caught (recall 0.004)
  • Only about 10% of encounters are early readmissions, so predicting "no" every time scores 90%. I rebalanced with SMOTE and judged models on recall and F1.
  • The strongest length-of-stay predictors (number of medications, lab procedures, discharge disposition) are only known by the end of the stay. Using them predicts the past, so I restricted the model to information available at admission and accepted the lower score.
  • The diagnosis columns are raw ICD-9 codes. I grouped them using the paper that published the dataset (Strack et al., 2014) and kept only each patient's first visit, since repeat stays may follow different patterns.
  • Clustering found no clear structure, which helped explain the ceiling. My conclusion for hospitals: the HbA1c test is missing for most patients, and collecting it consistently would do more than a better model.

Y1·C

OCUTRACK: eye-state detection

The brief: build an image classifier on a dataset you collect yourself. I chose open versus closed eyes, as the base for blink tracking in driver-fatigue and eye-strain applications. The dataset turned into a project of its own.

WhenFeb – Apr 2025
TeamIndividual
FocusVision, fairness, UX
The challenge

Build an eye-state classifier on a dataset I had to collect myself.

My approach

Turned ~300 videos into ~150,000 eye crops, labelled them with an active-learning loop, removed near-duplicates, then iterated CNNs and audited the model for bias.

Achievement

A working web app that tracks blinks on a live webcam, and a clear picture of which groups the model needed more data for.

Building the dataset
  1. ~300 videospeople facing a camera
  2. ~150,000 eye cropsMediaPipe Face Mesh, left and right
  3. Active learningauto-label at ≥95% confidence, retrain every 25 videos
  4. Deduplicateperceptual hashing removes ~99%
  5. Open / closedcompact, varied dataset
  • Model iterations from an MLP baseline through CNNs with augmentation and class weights to transfer learning, with error analysis at each step.
  • A bias audit: the data under-represented darker skin tones, East Asian eye shapes and children, and the model was failing on East Asian faces. For a drowsiness alert, that means the system is least safe for exactly the drivers it saw least.
  • A working Flask web app that runs the model on a live webcam feed and reports blink rate. A think-aloud study with users led me to split the camera view and the results into separate screens.

Same block, side challenge: a retrieval-augmented chatbot for an events company. It runs a local Llama 3.1 model, OCRs scanned brochures, and is prompted to say it doesn't know rather than guess.

Y2·A

Emotion in dialogue

Classifying the emotion of each line in TV-show transcripts. We went from Naive Bayes and SVM baselines to a fine-tuned DistilBERT, compared it with prompting a large language model, ran error analysis, explained predictions token by token with layer-wise relevance propagation, added a translation step and wrote a model card. My focus was the analysis side: error analysis and explainability.

WhenSep – Oct 2025
TeamTeam project
FocusNLP
The challenge

Classify the emotion of each line in TV-show dialogue, where the meaning depends on what came before.

My approach

Moved from classic baselines to a fine-tuned DistilBERT and LLM prompting, then used error analysis and token-level explanations to see where and why it failed.

Achievement

Showed why sentence-level models hit a ceiling on this task and proposed a model with contextual memory as the research direction.

The open question I left with. Emotion in a story depends on what happened earlier. A line like "well, that went perfectly" means the opposite after a loss. A model that reads one sentence at a time can't see that, whatever its size. What the task needs is a model with contextual memory: one that keeps track of earlier passages and reads each line in that light. I'd like to research that properly.
